Alice has a winning strategy exactly when at least one of nnn and kkk is odd. We first prove that Bob wins when both are even.

The even-even game

Pair the holes as

(1,2),(3,4),…,(n−1,n).(1,2),(3,4),\ldots,(n-1,n).(1,2),(3,4),…,(n−1,n).

Call a configuration good if each pair contains either two pegs or no pegs. Since kkk is even, the starting configuration is good.

Suppose Alice moves from a good configuration. Her source lies in a full pair and her destination in an empty pair. Bob moves the other peg of the source pair to the other hole of the destination pair. This is legal: Alice's destination is to the right of her source, so the entire destination pair is to the right of the entire source pair. Bob's move restores a good configuration.

Thus Bob always has a response. The game must terminate because the sum of occupied hole numbers increases on every move. Therefore Bob makes the last move and wins whenever nnn and kkk are both even.

Alice's first moves in the other cases

In each remaining parity class, Alice makes one move that leaves Bob to move first in a smaller even-even game.

  • If nnn and kkk are both odd, Alice moves the peg in hole kkk to hole nnn. That peg and the last hole are thereafter out of play, leaving the (n−1,k−1)(n-1,k-1)(n−1,k−1)-game, whose parameters are even.
  • If nnn is odd and kkk is even, Alice moves the peg in hole 111 to hole k+1k+1k+1. Hole 111 can never be used again, and holes 2,…,n2,\ldots,n2,…,n now begin with kkk occupied holes. This is the even-even (n−1,k)(n-1,k)(n−1,k)-game.
  • If nnn is even and kkk is odd, Alice moves the peg in hole 111 to hole nnn. Delete the unusable first hole and the fixed last peg with its hole. Holes 2,…,n−12,\ldots,n-12,…,n−1 form the even-even (n−2,k−1)(n-2,k-1)(n−2,k−1)-game.

Alice now uses the even-even pairing strategy as the second player in the reduced game. Hence her winning cases are precisely

n is odd or k is odd.\boxed{n\text{ is odd or }k\text{ is odd}.}n is odd or k is odd.​
